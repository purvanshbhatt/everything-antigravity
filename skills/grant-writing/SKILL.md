# Grant Writing

Reviewers score proposals against published criteria, often quickly and in
batches. Make each criterion easy to find and easy to score, keep every claim
backed, and never let a formatting rule disqualify good work.

## When to use

- Research grants (NIH, NSF, ERC, national councils), foundation and nonprofit grants, government innovation programs, open-source and public-interest technology funds.
- Turning a project idea into a specific-aims page or a letter of intent.
- Resubmissions that must answer reviewer critiques.

## When not to use

- Investor fundraising: `pitch-deck`, `investor-updates`.
- Business strategy documents: `business-plan`.
- The background literature itself: `literature-review` (then cite its output here).

## Workflow

1. **Dissect the call.** Read the full solicitation (RFA, NOFO, call text) and build a compliance checklist: eligibility, deadlines with time zone, page and character limits, fonts and margins, required sections and attachments, budget caps, allowable costs, submission portal and registration lead times. Registration with some portals takes weeks; check now.

   **Check:** A checklist table exists with every "must" from the call and a status column.

2. **Test funder fit.** Map the project to the funder's stated priorities and review criteria, using their own language. Read recently funded awards. If the call lists a program officer, draft questions for the user to send (sending is the user's decision).

   **Check:** Each review criterion maps to a section where it will be addressed.

3. **Write the core page first.** For research: the specific-aims page (problem and gap, long-term goal, objective, central hypothesis, two or three aims with expected outcomes, impact). For nonprofits: a one-page summary with need, intervention, outcomes and a logic model (inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, impact).

   **Check:** A reviewer reading only this page can state the gap, the plan and why this team.

4. **Draft to the criteria.** Use the funder's criterion names as headings where allowed. Significance: why the problem matters, with cited evidence. Innovation: what is new relative to the state of the art. Approach: methods per aim, preliminary data, expected results, potential problems with alternative strategies, timeline. Team and environment: why these people and resources are sufficient.

   **Check:** Every aim has methods, a measurable outcome, a risk and a fallback.

5. **Build the budget and justification.** Personnel with effort, fringe, equipment, travel, materials, participant costs, subawards, and indirect costs at the organization's negotiated or the funder's allowed rate (from the user's grants office, never assumed). The justification ties each cost to an aim.

   **Check:** Budget totals match across forms, and nothing exceeds the cap or uses a disallowed category.

6. **Plan milestones and evaluation.** A timeline (Gantt-style table) with quarterly milestones and deliverables, plus an evaluation plan with indicators, data sources and who measures.

   **Check:** Each milestone is verifiable by someone outside the team.

7. **Mark gaps instead of inventing.** Preliminary data, letters of support, citations and institutional figures come from the user. Use visible placeholders such as `[TBD: pilot enrolment numbers from user]` and list them for the user.

   **Check:** No fabricated data, quotes, letters or references; the placeholder list is empty or handed to the user.

8. **Review against the checklist and a mock panel.** Score the draft against the criteria as a reviewer would, fix weaknesses, run the `editing` pass, then verify the compliance checklist again. Submission is the user's action; prepare files and confirm before anything is uploaded.

   **Check:** Every checklist item is green, and the user has confirmed the submission package.

## Reference

Common criteria (verify against the current call; funders revise them):

| Funder type | Typical criteria |
| :--- | :--- |
| NSF | Intellectual Merit; Broader Impacts |
| NIH | Simplified framework for many applications from 2025: importance of the research, rigor and feasibility, expertise and resources |
| Horizon Europe | Excellence; Impact; Quality and efficiency of implementation |
| Foundations, nonprofits | Mission fit, need, outcomes, organizational capacity, sustainability, budget reasonableness |
| Open-source funds | Public benefit, maintenance need, feasibility, team track record, milestones |

Resubmission introduction pattern: thank reviewers briefly, group critiques by theme, state the change made for each and where it appears (page and section), and disagree respectfully with evidence where warranted.

## Anti-patterns

| Don't | Do instead | Why |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Write the narrative before reading the call | Build the compliance checklist first | Format violations can mean rejection without review |
| Hide the aims in prose | Number them and repeat their names consistently | Reviewers score what they can find |
| Promise everything | Scope to the budget and timeline | Over-ambition reads as infeasible |
| Assume an indirect cost rate | Get it from the grants office | Wrong rates break budgets late |
| Invent preliminary data or letters | Placeholders and a list for the user | Fabrication is misconduct |

## Verification checklist

- [ ] Compliance checklist complete and all green.
- [ ] Every review criterion is addressed under a findable heading.
- [ ] Aims have methods, measurable outcomes, risks and alternatives.
- [ ] Budget matches forms, caps and allowed categories; justification maps to aims.
- [ ] Timeline milestones are verifiable.
- [ ] No fabricated content; placeholders handed to the user; submission confirmed by the user.
